The low-water rate spike came two months early

Barge freight on the lower Mississippi jumped 24 percent in the last full week of July, a move this river usually saves for late September.

Ken Beegle · published August 3, 2026 · figures as of July 28, 2026
Topics: Rates, River conditions, Low water, Grain

Update, August 4

Two things have moved since this was published, and they point in opposite directions. No rate figure has changed: USDA has not printed a new week, so the $18.09 a ton for the week reported July 28 and everything derived from it stands.

The forecast got deeper. The guidance cited above was issued August 1. The issuances of August 2 and 3 carry St. Louis to −0.1 ft rather than 1.0 ft, putting it below gauge zero, and Memphis to −1.6 ft rather than −1.0 ft, both on August 17. Vicksburg eased slightly, to 11.5 ft on August 24. In all three the lowest point is still the last point.

The observed river has stopped falling. St. Louis stage since the July 28 reading has oscillated rather than declined: 3.80, 3.16, 2.32, 2.83, 3.54, 3.03, and 2.82 ft on August 3. Discharge there is flat at 124 to 130 thousand cubic feet per second. Downstream is still draining, but more slowly than the headline suggests: Memphis discharge fell 23 percent over those six days and Vicksburg 10 percent.

So the market is currently pricing a forecast that observation has not yet confirmed. That is the strongest available counterargument to the piece above, and it is worth stating plainly: if St. Louis holds near 3 ft rather than crossing zero, the October premium described here is priced off a path the river did not take. The next two guidance cycles and Thursday's USDA print are the tests.

A July move in a September market

The seasonal pattern is one-sided. Across the thirteen years of the matched rate-and-river record, the twelve largest weekly increases in any month all fall between August 20 and October 11, with a single exception in March 2022. The big rate move on this river is a harvest-season animal. This one showed up in July.

The two recent low-water years make the calendar shift concrete. Measured at a common threshold, the first week the lower-river average reached $14 a ton, 2026 crossed in the week reported July 21. In 2022 the crossing came the week of August 30, and in 2023 the week of August 29: five weeks later, in both years. From there, 2022 ran to a peak of $87.19 on October 11 and 2023 to $51.62 on September 26.

The river fell fourteen feet in four weeks

The driver is the river, and the river moved fast. The Mississippi at St. Louis fell from 17.3 feet on June 30 to 3.3 feet on July 28 (U.S. Geological Survey gauge readings), while discharge dropped 56 percent, from 287,000 to 127,000 cubic feet per second. July's minimum stage at St. Louis was the second-lowest July reading in the 2013 to 2026 record, behind only drought-year 2023.

And the forecast points down, not up. National Weather Service guidance issued August 1 carries St. Louis to 1.0 foot and Memphis to a foot below gauge zero by around August 16, and Vicksburg to 10.8 feet by August 23. In all three forecasts the lowest point is the last point: the guidance runs out before the river finds a bottom. Those are forecasts, not measurements, and they extend only two to three weeks; but nothing in the current guidance shows a turn.

The forward market has already voted

USDA publishes forward barge quotes as a percentage of a 1976 benchmark tariff, and in the July 28 week every origin region was quoted above spot for October delivery: 44 percent above from the Cairo to Memphis stretch, 42 percent from the Ohio origins, 38 percent from St. Louis, and 25 to 28 percent from the Illinois and upper river.

Shippers appear to be responding to the same math. Grain tonnage through the seven locks USDA tracks hit 2.37 million tons in the week ending July 25, the highest weekly total since at least early May, consistent with grain moving early, ahead of the drawdown, though the tonnage data alone cannot prove the motive.

What would make this wrong

Two things this is not. It is not 2022: at $18.09, rates stand at roughly one-fifth of the 2022 peak, and the river itself is not yet at crisis stage. Memphis read 0.85 feet on the morning of August 2, still well above the minus 8-foot mark the Weather Service treats as its low-water threshold there, and nowhere near the record lows of roughly minus 11 feet in October 2022 and minus 12 feet in October 2023.

And it is not a price forecast. The market is pricing a river forecast, and river forecasts unwind: two weeks of wet weather across the Missouri and Ohio basins would take the floor out from under the October quotes. One caution on reading the number itself: these are grain-barge rates, the standard public signal for shared-channel capacity. A tank barge or project cargo prices differently, even if it competes for the same water.
